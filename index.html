Gene Regulatory Network Inference in the Presence of Selection Bias and Latent Confounders

NeurIPS 2025 · View paper

1. Assumptions: Gene expression is shaped by three classes of influence: regulation by TFs, latent factors, and selection-induced constraints (e.g., cellular viability)

Under these assumptions, the conditional dependencies observed among genes may arise from three distinct causal mechanisms: regulation, latent confounding, and selection.

Regulation

TFs can directly modulate the expression of their target genes. These directed regulatory relationships generate dependencies between regulators and their targets.

X → Y

Latent confounding

Unobserved regulatory factors, such as non-coding RNAs, epigenetic states, or environmental signals, may jointly influence multiple genes. Their shared effects can induce dependencies even when no direct regulatory relationship exists between the genes.

X ← L → Y

Selection

Evolutionary and cellular selection constrains which gene-expression profiles are viable. Conditioning on survival, sampling, or inclusion can induce dependencies between genes that would otherwise be independent.

X → S ← Y   (observe S = 1)

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive: regulation, latent confounding, and selection may coexist for the same gene pair.

Conditional Dependence Does Not Imply a Regulatory Relationship

For genes with available perturbation data, GISL (our method) attributes the conditional dependency between each gene pair to its underlying source: regulation, latent confounding, or selection. The PDF below illustrates dependencies attributed to selection-induced biological constraints, such as cellular survival.

The effect of selection on a gene can be assessed using a Z-score, which quantifies the change in cellular growth following perturbation. Intuitively, perturbing a gene under strong selection constraints may disrupt a condition required for cellular survival or proliferation, leading to a larger-magnitude Z-score.
